On Wednesday April 16 the Li -
mon Fire Board discussed the 
upcoming election of three of the 
positions on the board that are up 
for vote. President, Treasurer, and 
Secretary positions are current -
ly up for voting. The members 
of the community that are run -
ning for these positions are Kurt 
Thompson, Teresa O’Dwyer, Bri -
anne Harlow, Andrew Lorensen, 
Tim Rogers, and Justin Rhodes. 
Kurt Thompson is the current Fire 
Board President, Teresa O’Dwyer 
is the current Treasurer, and Bri -
anne Harlow in the current Secre -
tary. 
It is very important that everyone 
gets to the Limon Fire Station to 
vote on May 6th, voting can only 
be done in person at the fire sta -
tion. 
Tim Anderson with Gordon In -
surance reviewed the current in -
surance policies that the Limon 
Fire Department has and discussed 
the changes to the buildings that 
are being made and wanted to 
make sure that things were cov -
ered on the policy's as needed.
During the Chiefs report discus -
sion was had about putting in a new 
flagpole along with the instillation 
of a new sign that is being donat -
ed. Discussion about bring back an 
old favorite fundraiser for the fire 
department. The department once 
held a street dance allowing the 
community to attend and support 
its local department. The board did 
make a motion to move forward 
with the plans for the street dance 
fundraiser that would be held at 
the fire department. The board

ry for their help with the alcohol.

Chief Perry also made the board

of the volunteer members are at -

which is a Friday- Sunday training

that the department has responded 
to 95 calls already this year. She 
also stated that they were assisting

A brief update was given about

By Fred Lundy
On April 14, 2025, at 7:00pm, 
the Hugo Town Board met for a 
regularly scheduled meeting. At 
roll call, all members were pres -
ent. During the public comment 
period, a resident stated that their 
sewer line had an issue and had 
called the town staff to check the 
main line, located in the alley 
between Fourth Street and Fifth 
street behind their residence. The 
town staff determined the line was 
clear and that the issue must have 
been in the residence.  It was later 
determined that the sewer line tap 
had failed. This tap was installed 
during the project on the town 
sewer line, with the work being 
done by outside contractors. The 
resident requested that the town 
reimburse the cost of the plumb -
er that they had paid. The Board 
agreed to follow up with GMS, 
the engineering company, to try to 
remedy the situation. 
Next was a public hearing for a 
minor subdivision replat for a res -
ident. With no public comment or 
objections, the request was granted. 
A discussion was then had on 
a project on the sewer lines that 
would help to find leaks and issues 
in the infrastructure. This would 
involve the piping of smoke into 
the system to be used as an indica-
tor of the presence of issues. The 
Boards main concern in this proj -
ect was public awareness of the 
project as it occurred. 
The next item was to set a date 
for a meeting with the town’s code 
compliance officer, Susie Ellis of 
Community Preservation Spe -
cialists, Inc. Trustee Todd Messer 
made a statement to the effect that 
he did not feel that a meeting was 
necessary at this time. He indicat -
ed that he was disappointed in the 
performance of CPS, and that he 
felt that the termination of the con-
tract should be discussed. After a 
lengthy conversation, a date was 
set for Thursday, June 19 at 6:30 
pm for a meeting with Susie Ellis. 
This is a public meeting and public 
participation is welcomed. 
Gillian Laycock of Hugo Main 
Street reported that the funding for 
the parking lot in the park project 
had been secured had that part of 
the project could move forward. 
She also spoke of additional fund-
ing for Main Street businesses that 
had been received and that those 
projects were ongoing.  
The public portion closed for an 
executive session for a personnel 
matter. 
For more information on this 
meeting, please contact the Hugo 
Town Hall. 
Hugo Town Board meeting
(Editor's note: Please see the Town 
of Arriba website for complete 
meeting minutes)
Mayor Marilyn Lightsey called the 
meeting to order at 6:00 p.m., with 
most trustees present except for Car-
olyn Steinsiek. Clerk Josie Hart was 
also in attendance, and no members 
of the public were present.
Erica Saffer provided an up -
date on the Hugo Hospital, noting 
concerns about possible federal 
Medicaid funding cuts and their 
impact on care and finances. She 
also shared that grants had en -
abled the hospital to purchase new 
equipment. The board unanimous-
ly approved the meeting agenda, 
February minutes, and February's 
accounts receivable and payable. 
January sales tax revenue totaled 
$2,176.20. An additional payment 
of $1,000 to Leisure Pines for a 
laptop was added to March bills, 
which were also approved.
A street parking ordinance was ad-
opted, while a dumpster ordinance 
was tabled to allow time to consult 
with a local septic company. A zon-
ing workshop was scheduled for 
April 25. The Water Vault Project 
was declared complete, and pay -
ments to contractors Glacier and 
GMS were approved, with some 
costs covered by a DOLA grant.
GMS was officially designat -
ed as the engineer for the town’s 
wastewater permit project. The 
board approved an $87,500 agree-
ment with GMS to assist with 
permit compliance, and a DOLA 
grant application will be submitted 
to cover half the cost.
Water and sewer rates were both 
increased. The base water rate 
rose from $43.50 to $49.50, and 
the minimum usage included was 
raised from 4,000 to 5,000 gallons. 
Sewer rates were increased as well: 
in-town residential rates went from 
$44 to $47.50, out-of-town rates 
from $67.50 to $71.00, and the 
CDOT Rest Area fee from $1,400 
to $2,000, effective April 1, 2025. 
These changes were necessary to 
meet compliance and funding re -
quirements related to new waste -
water discharge regulations.
Trustee Siobhan Steinsiek report-
ed someone is interested in help -
ing with the community garden, 
and the board approved $300 for 
its support. Clerk Hart was autho -
rized to spend $350 on supplies for 
the town’s Easter Egg Hunt sched-
uled for April 19. In the mainte -
nance report, Clerk Hart and the 
mayor noted that Mike Becker has 
been busy with wastewater test -
ing and tree trimming. Trustees 
discussed equipment rentals and 
cost-sharing for tree and concrete 
management. The mayor had no 
new updates.
The meeting adjourned at 8:26 
p.m. and the minutes were official-
ly approved by the board on April 
15, 2025.

Written By Gigen Mammoser
A new study suggests that pro -
biotics can benefit mental health 
even in generally healthy popu -
lations and may reduce negative 
mood, according to a new clinical 
trial.  The study adds more ev -
idence to the gut-brain connec -
tion’s role in regulating emotion.  
Experts caution that probiotics 
are not a substitute for psychiatric 
medication and therapy.  
In the randomized, double-blind, 
placebo-controlled study, research-
ers observed that participants be -
gan feeling mood improvements 
within about two weeks of taking 
probiotics.
Because the study involved 
healthy adults — not just people 
with diagnosed depression — the 
findings suggest probiotics could 
benefit mental health across the 
general population.  The research-
ers noted their use of daily mood 
tracking may have helped detect 
subtle improvements that other 
mental health assessments could 
miss.  
The study adds to growing inter-
est in the gut-brain connection, a 
complex communication system 
that is thought to influence mood 
and emotions between the gut 
and the brain.  The gut produces a 
large amount of serotonin (about 
95%), a neurotransmitter involved 
in everything from mood to appe -
tite. This suggests that improving 
gut health could positively impact 
mental health.  Researchers con -
ducted a comprehensive study in -
volving 88 healthy volunteers with 
an average age of 22. Participants 
in the study were not overweight, 
which was based on their body 
mass index (BMI). They also did 
not engage in excessive drinking, 
nor drug use.  Once enrolled, par -
ticipants were randomly assigned 
to receive either a multispecies 
probiotic mixture or a placebo for 
28 days.  The probiotic mixture 
contained nine bacterial strains, 
including various Bifidobacte -
rium, Lactobacillus, and Lacto -
coccus species. The researchers 
administered a comprehensive 
battery of validated psychological 
questionnaires before and after the 
four-week intervention, including 
measures of anxiety (STAI), wor -
ry (PSWQ), depression (CES-D), 
and negative affect (PANAS).
Despite this thorough approach, 
these standard psychological as -
sessments detected minimal dif -
ferences between the probiotic and 
placebo groups. However, daily 
mood reporting told a different 
story.  This daily monitoring re -
vealed the study’s most significant 
finding: participants who received 
probiotics showed a reduction in 
negative mood starting around 
the two-week mark, while those 
on placebo showed no such im -
provement.  “By monitoring peo -
ple daily, rather than just assessing 
them before and after, it gives us a 
much better resolution of changes 
over time when taking probiotics,” 
Johnson said.  “One disadvantage 
with psychological questionnaires 
is that they attempt to dissect how 
people are feeling into discrete cat-
egories, e.g., stress, anger, anxiety, 
or depressive tendencies. In con -
trast, asking how positive or neg -
ative someone is feeling can ho -
listically capture a change in any 
emotional state,” she explained.
These findings suggest that pro -
biotics can benefit mental health 
even in generally healthy popula -
tions and that common research 
methods may miss these effects. 
Interestingly, the improvements 
in negative mood didn’t affect 
positive mood, which remained 
unchanged in both groups.  Ac -
cording to the study authors, if 
probiotics could simultaneously 
reduce negative mood without re -
ducing positive mood, that would 
prove to be a “notable benefit.” 
The researchers also found that 
certain individuals might respond 
better to probiotics than others. 
Specifically, as measured by psy -
chological questionnaires, indi -
viduals with higher baseline risk 
aversion showed the greatest im -
provement in negative mood when 
taking probiotics.  Despite the 
promising results, experts caution 
that probiotics are not a substitute 
for psychiatric medication or ther-
apy.
Probiotics may be a helpful ad -
dition to a broader mental health 
strategy, but they shouldn’t replace 
proven treatments for conditions 
like depression or anxiety.  “The 
results from this study underline 
that probiotics are certainly not 
sufficient as a standalone solution 
in the prevention and treatment of 
mental illness,” Christoph Thaiss, 
PhD, assistant professor of pathol-
ogy at Stanford University.  “It is 
also important to remember that 
probiotics are available as nutri -
tional supplements that do not un -
dergo the same rigorous testing for 
efficacy as FDA-approved drugs,” 
he added.  Still have questions?

macists at Hoffman Drug, you 
might want to pick up a probiotic 
while you’re there!

ENJOYING LOCAL EVENTS
I conduct seminars at most of 
the big outdoor shows around the 
country but I’m not too sure that 
I don’t enjoy small local events 
better. They’re lower key and you 
have time to really talk to people. 
Plus, they have all of the good 
food. Kettle corn, flavored cotton 
candy and so forth. I was reminded 
of this today when I hit the Rugged 
Idaho Expo at the Indian Creek 
Plaza.
Every small show that I hit, I’m 
always shocked at how many local 
outdoor companies that I meet that 
I never knew existed. And they’re 
right here under my nose. I thought 
that I was pretty well connected 
but I guess not. Let’s go over some 
of the booths that interested me.
The first booth that I hit was M 
& M Jigs located in Wilder. Two 
young men named Morgan and 
Micheal Etter were setting at the 
table and their mom and dad were 
in the background. They greeted 
me and asked what kind of fishing 
that I liked to do. I saw that they 
had some crappie jigs and crappie 
fishing should kick off within a 
few days (By the time you’re read-
ing this article) so I told them I like 
crappie fishing.
They were very good little busi -
ness men and answered all of my 
questions. (They’re only 12 & 9 
years old)! The mom suggested 
that they have me test out a couple 
of jigs and see how they worked 
out. If you see a cooler full of crap-
pie in my next article then you’ll 
know which jigs to use!
As we were finishing up their 
dad stepped up and gave me a lit -
tle history. Turns up that they’re 
in business with their grandad. 
He makes the jigs and the boys tie 
on the bucktails and are working 
the shows to promote them. How 
cool is that? A grandad starting his 
grandsons off in business together. 
Love it.
I met Steve Weston years ago 
when we were both conduct -
ing seminars at Seirra Trading 
Post and Cabela’s and have been 
friends since. He is an awesome 
chef and was conducting back -
country cooking seminars when 
I met him. It didn’t take long for 
me to figure out that it’d be smart 
to set-up my table beside him, that 
way I could be the official sampler. 
He has done nothing but gotten 
better since those days.
I had noticed on the event sched-
ule that he was going to be con -
ducting a Backcountry Cooking 
seminar at this event so Katy and 
I made it a point to attend. As is 
usual, I learned a lot of new tips 
from him.
He published a cooking book 
titled IN THE WILD CHEF. I’ve 
got one years ago and really enjoy 
it. He simplifies the recipes and yet 
you will feel like you are eating 
gourmet meals. I’d recommend 
that you buy this book to liven up 
your backcountry meals. I won’t 
do him justice but many mountain 
climbing and backcountry expedi-
tions hire him to be their chef. 
Clinton Miller had a booth set-
up and has an outfit named Sage 
to Sawtooth Bushcraft and is an 
expert at building fires. He too had 
a seminar but I got held up and ar-
rived just as he was finishing up. 
I did get to talk to him for a bit 
though and he shared some tips.
One tip I’d never heard of. He 
said the better guitar picks are su -
per flammable and make a good 
fire starter. Never knew that. He 
also had a flat piece of plastic, 
about the size as a credit card 
that acts as magnifying glass with 
which he can start a fire. Someday 
hopefully I can attend one of his 
seminars.
I stopped at one booth named AE-
GIS Gun Care which has gun oils, 
lubricants and kits with brushes 
and rifle/pistols cables. I’m going 
to have to check them out.
Then I also stopped at two booths 
that offer holsters. Havok Holsters 
and Tactical Concealment Holster. 
I’m going to have to check them 
out as well. I’m always looking for 
the perfect holster, aren’t you?
We live in an outdoor state and 
consequently have a lot of outdoor 
companies here. You’d of thought 
that by now that I knew all of them 
but apparently not! I’d encourage 
you to hit the little shows, Expos 
and such. You never know what 
kind of new products you might 
find or the interesting people that 
you might meet.
Morgan and Micheal are quite the little businessmen. They had a booth 
set up selling crappie jigs.
In The Wild Chef Steve Weston put on a great Backcountry Cooking 
seminar.

Did you know …which is the 
oldest settlement in the United 
States?  Forget Jamestown. Forget 
Roanoke.  Forget St. Augustine. 
According to book learning, the 
oldest settlement in the United 
States is Acoma Pueblo, which sits 
on top of a 365-foot-high mesa in 
the middle of New Mexico.
  It will come as no surprise that 
Native American settlements pre -
date European ones, but it may 
surprise some people that Acoma 
Pueblo, west of Albuquerque, New 
Mexico, has been continuously 
occupied since the 12th century. 
The Acoma still inhabit their “Sky 
City,” a settlement of about 4,800 
people. Traditionally hunters and 
traders, the Acoma people now 
make their income from a cultural 
center and casino complex. Coin -
cidentally, the oldest state capital 
in the United States is Santa Fe, 
which recently celebrated its 400th 
anniversary.
  Now that’s what it says in the 
fact book, but it’s quite common 
for those fact guys to not consid -
er Alaska to be part of the United 
States. I can recall many times 
when I was freezing to death in 
Alaska at 40 below and the radio 
told me the coldest place in the na-
tion was some town in Minnesota 
with 10 below.
 Why do I mention this? Because, 
according to two archaeologists I 
interviewed for the Anchorage 
Daily News, the tiny Athabascan 
Indian village of Nulato on the Yu-
kon River has been continuously 
inhabited since the end of the last 
Ice Age  … about 10,000 years 
ago.
Brought to you by the wonder -
ful people who live in Kwethluk, 
Alaska, because they haven’t been 
there as long as their friends in 
Nulato, but they still say “forever” 
when asked.
By Joy Johnson
My name is Joy Johnson and I 
am the Veterans Service Officer 
for Lincoln and Washington Coun-
ties. My job is to help veterans file 
for benefits with the US Depart -
ment of Veterans Affairs. There 
is no charge to the veteran for my 
services.
It has come to my attention that 
not all veterans know that they 
may be entitled to V A benefits 
after they leave the military. The 
most important benefit is disability 
compensation. If you were injured 
while in the service and still have 
issues from that injury, you may 
be eligible for disability compen -
sation. Some examples are your 
knees and back were damaged 
from jumping out of planes or he -
licopters; you broke your shoulder 
during PT and it just isn't right; 
you experienced combat and you 
have PTSD. You must be able to 
prove that you were injured during 
service and have a current diagno-
sis. 
Another common benefit is edu -
cation. You may qualify for tuition 
assistance depending on when you 
served. There are several programs 
Veterans--Back to Basics
available. 
V A Pension is a program for 
low-income veterans. There are 
net worth requirements that must 
be met. 
A veteran may be entitled to 
burial benefits. ALL deceased vet-
erans are entitled to an American 
flag, a Presidential certificate, a 
headstone and a medallion. Some 
veterans may be entitled to be 
buried in a national cemetery. It's 
very important to make your wish-
es known to family and for them 
to know where your DD214 is. I 
can help you apply for pre-need 
eligibility to determine if you're 
eligible to be buried in a national 
cemetery.
As a veteran you may be eligible 
for a V A home loan. A V A home 
loan is backed by the V A. You may 
also be eligible for home improve-
ments due to a service-connected 
disability such a wheelchair ramp 
or other accommodations. 
If you need more information 
or have questions, don't hesitate 
to contact me at 719 892 4800. 
For Lincoln County veterans, you 
can email vso@lincolncountyco.
us; for Washington County vet -
erans, you can email me at vso@
co.washington.co.us.

Lincoln  (April 2025) — April 
30 is the last day to apply for Col -
orado’s Low-income Energy As -
sistance Program (LEAP). LEAP 
helps vulnerable Coloradans pay 
a portion of winter home heating 
costs so they have more money to 
spend on other essentials like food, 
medicine and rent. Nearly 80,000 
eligible Coloradans have received 
an average benefit of $355 from 
LEAP this heating season. 
It’s not too late for Lincoln Coun-
ty residents to apply for LEAP!  
We encourage anyone who thinks 
they may be eligible to apply.
To qualify, Coloradans may have 
an income up to 60 percent of the 
state median income, which is a 
household income of less than 
$78,060 a year for a family of four. 
Additionally, LEAP recipients 
must pay home heating costs di -
rectly to a utility company or land-
lord as part of their rent and have 
at least one U.S. citizen or perma -
nent legal resident of the U.S. liv -
ing in the household.
To apply for LEAP, visit CDHS’s 
website (cdhs.colorado.com/leap). 
Online applications are processed 
through the Colorado PEAK sys -
tem. You can also call the HEAT 
HELP line at 1-866-HEAT-HELP 
(1-866-432-8435) to receive an 
application via mail, phone or 
email, or you can visit your Park 
County Department of Human 
Services office to pick up or drop 
off an application.
The Colorado Low-income En -
ergy Assistance Program (LEAP) 
is a federally funded program that 
helps eligible hard-working Colo -
rado families, seniors and individ-
uals pay a portion of their winter 
home heating costs. 
LHEAP was founded in 1981. 
The Colorado Low-income En -
ergy Assistance Program (LEAP) 
is a federally funded program that 
helps eligible hard-working Colo -
rado families, seniors and individ-
uals pay a portion of their winter 
home heating costs. Our goal is to 
help bring warmth, comfort and 
safety to your home and family by 
assisting with heating costs.
The LEAP program works to 
keep our communities warm 
during the winter (November 
through April) by providing as -
sistance with heating costs, equip-
ment repair and/or replacement of 
inoperable heating tools. While 
the program is not intended to pay 
the entire cost of home heating, we 
aim to help alleviate some of the 
burdens that come with Colorado's 
colder months.
In most cases, the energy assis -
tance benefit is paid directly to 
the household energy supplier. In 
most cases, if you are approved 
for LEAP, payments are made di -
rectly to your primary heating fuel 
vendor and a notice will be sent 
to you informing you of the ben -
efit amount. Other benefits pro -
vided by LEAP include repair or 
replacement of a home’s primary 
heating system, such as a furnace 
or wood-burning stove. The pro -
gram does not provide financial 
assistance for any type of tempo -
rary or portable heating.
Did you know LEAP is approved 
for income up to 60% of the state 
median income level?

Question: “Have you been to the 
Hedlund House Museum lately?” 
Reply: “Where is the Hedlund 
House Museum?”  
Answer: “In Hugo.”  
Question: “There is a Museum in 
Hugo?!” 
Answer: “Yes! The Hedlund 
House Museum, located in Hugo, 
is a jewel, filled with the history  of 
Lincoln County. It is available for 
research, and hosts several events 
a year. Tours are  currently avail -
able by appointment.” 
This is an example of a typical 
conversation, in regards to the 
Hedlund House Museum,  located 
at 617 3rd Avenue, in Hugo.  
The Hedlund House was do -
nated to the Town of Hugo, by 
Mertie Hedlund in the 1970s. The  
purpose of the donation was for 
the House to serve as the Lincoln 
County Historical Museum, and 
house the collection of the Lincoln 
County Historical Society. 
The Museum  houses an exten -
sive collection of photos, news -
paper articles, scrapbooks and 
artifacts of  Lincoln County’s rich 
past. For many residents, the mu -
seum houses links to their family  
history. Donations from individu -
als and families, of historic cloth -
ing, furniture, musical  instruments 
and other ephemera, line the walls 
and are tucked away in every nook 
and cranny  of the two story home, 
as well as the adjacent carriage 
house, coal house, and garage.  
While there was once a lively 
group of volunteers that were on-
site, and able to offer daily tours  
of the museum, modern life does 
not easily accommodate this type 
of schedule. With most  families 
having to rely on two incomes, to 
survive, volunteerism has seen a 
drastic decrease.  Sheri Gaskins, 
President of the Lincoln County 
Historical Society, a local business 
owner, is  currently, the only muse-
um volunteer, following the depar-
ture of Secretary/Treasurer; Janelle  
Fox-Lausier, necessitating appoint-
ments to view the Hedlund House.  
Since taking the helm of the 
Hedlund House three years ago, 
Gaskins and Fox-Lausier have  
worked hard to bring the museum 
to life by hosting school tours and  
a number of  events. 
Janelle’s departure, while a defi-
nite loss to the Lincoln County 
Historical Society and  Hedlund 
House Museum, has not slowed 
the determination of Sheri Gas -
kins to make the  Hedlund House 
Museum into a thriving, and vital 
element of Lincoln County.
